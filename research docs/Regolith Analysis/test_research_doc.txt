The goal of the Regolith Analysis was to analyze Martian soil(called regolith because of its lack of organic matter such as worms, insects or plants) and evaluate its suitability for the growing of food, as well as its general level of toxicity. Going into the experiment, we can assume that Mars soil would be suitable for growing most crops, from results of a previous experiment in 2016, where scientists had successful results in growing crops in replicated Martian regolith with cut up grass as fertilizer added to them; plants have also been successfully grown on the international space station. Another factor to consider could be that Martian soil has been confirmed to contain most of the macronutrients and micronutrients needed for the growth of plants.

From the results, we can immediately identify one major issue with growing crops in this regolith: the abundance of heavy metals, most importantly Calcium Perchlorate, which is a toxic salt that is dangerous if ingested in large quantities. This could make plants grown in this soil toxic and unsuitable for humans to eat. Perchlorate, however, can also be used to our advantage, as it has the ability to suck water out of the air, and can be used to produce oxygen if perchlorate eating bacteria are used. Perchlorates can be easily separated from soil by running water through it, and the water and perchlorates can be separated later, leading to no waste. The problem of lack of fertilizer can be solved by using feces, though it has to be treated first, as feces normally contains pathogens which can be harmful to the crops.

However, there is one problem that has to be worked around, which is the potential danger to human health from fine Martian dust. Commonly found silicates such as Olivine, Pyroxene and Feldspar can react with small amounts of water to produce highly reactive molecules, similar to the ones produced during the mining of quartz, and can lead to lung diseases such as cancer.
